RoboBelief: A Belief Bottleneck for Robot Policies That Share the World

When a person changes the scene while the robot is not watching, a VLA that carries an explicit probability over the hidden state decides far better than one that only remembers frames.

University of Michigan × MBZUAI (Prof. Xingxing Zuo's group), 2026 · X-VLA 0.9B with LoRA · LIBERO kitchen with a scripted human partner · pre-registered closed-loop experiments

A person opens a drawer, moves the bowl, closes it — while the robot looks elsewhere. Asked to fetch the bowl, the robot has to act on what probably happened. Today's VLAs see only the current frame or keep a memory of past frames. RoboBelief gives the policy an explicit belief: a calibrated probability over the hidden variable ("which drawer is the bowl in?"), updated as events arrive and fed into the action model. With the same backbone, data and budget, the belief policy opens the posterior-optimal drawer first in 87% of runs; every frame-memory variant stays at the level of a policy that ignores the history.

The testbed

A hidden state with an exact answer

In the LIBERO kitchen, a scripted partner acts between the robot's observations: it moves the bowl (in view or masked), touches drawers without changing anything, glimpses inside, or shows a drawer empty. Because the partner's generative model is known, the correct posterior over the bowl's drawer is computed exactly for every test cell (3,998 cells, maximum error about 10⁻¹⁶). A policy is scored on whether its first drawer is the posterior-optimal one (POFC) — a measure of the decision, not of eventually finding the bowl by search.

Result 1 · Decisions

Memory policies receive the evidence and do not use it.

At 16 partner acts, the 51 frames a RoboMME-style memory policy samples contain the decisive event in 96–100% of episodes. Its first choice is still at the level of the blind policy and the uniform floor. The belief policy keeps posterior-optimal choices at every horizon, including 64 acts it never trained on.

Result 2 · Which ingredient matters

Not the labels, not the recurrence — the probability in the action path.

The controls separate the three ingredients. Labels alone do not help: frame memory given the same hidden-state labels as an auxiliary head stays at 40%. Recurrence with labels does not either: a recurrent policy that reads the whole stream and is supervised on the same labels executes well but chooses at prior level — the belief policy beats it by +0.40 [0.31, 0.49] at 32 acts (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001). A label-free recurrent belief keeps moves it saw (0.87 on witnessed chains) but loses the partner's habit on masked moves: 0.10, against 0.70 for the belief bottleneck.

The policy also reads the belief causally. Replacing the posterior at query time while keeping every frame fixed, the first drawer follows the swapped-in belief in 179 of 180 runs. Feeding the history of a different scene moves the first choice to that scene's posterior in 75% of runs. One qualification we report: passing only the belief's top choice works as well on this task (88%), so the gain lies in forming the right belief, not in the policy using its fine-grained probabilities.

Result 3 · The belief itself

A calibrated posterior that updates like Bayes' rule

On 231 validation episodes the filter's distance from the exact posterior is KL 0.080 and Brier 0.037. A forecast that uses only the partner's habit is at 0.712 / 0.424, and the auxiliary label head of the best memory policy is at 6.52 / 0.981. When the robot finds a drawer empty, the filter redistributes the probability between the two remaining drawers as Bayes' rule does, to within 0.054 on average over 119 events.

How far the claim goes: the filter recipe is not yet stable across fits. Refitted with other seeds, the same recipe gives first-choice rates of 71–87% (mean 77%), and the 87% headline uses the best of those fits. Every fit still beats every frame-memory and label-supervised control by at least 24 points, and the label-free recurrent belief by at least 14. Most policies were trained with one seed, the memory baselines with about 1/32 of RoboMME's training budget, the partner is scripted, and everything is in simulation with at most three hidden states. All hypotheses were pre-registered before data collection: 34 held, 14 were partially supported, 9 were not confirmed, 2 were invalid.

The belief bottleneck

An element encoder feeds a GRU filter that runs over the whole session and emits three probabilities, one per drawer. The probabilities (as [log p, p]) enter the action transformer through four tokens. The filter (about 38 MB) is fitted first on exactly weighted ground-truth labels and then frozen while the policy is trained — which is what allows clamping or swapping the belief at test time. Every policy is a LoRA adapter (rank 32, α 64) of the same pinned X-VLA-Libero checkpoint, trained on the same demonstrations for 20k iterations.

Rules

Hypotheses, analysis plans and decision rules were fixed in hashed files before data collection; amendments are dated. Failed runs and seeds are excluded and reported, never replaced. Contrast intervals are 95% cluster-bootstrap intervals over the 10 evaluation scene seeds.

Next

Running: two partners with mirrored habits (does the belief read who acted? — the current filter does not use actor identity), three-way identity among identical bowls, and masked container swaps. Planned: memory baselines retrained at RoboMME scale with three seeds, filter stabilization (ensembles, selection against the exact posterior), a π0.5 backbone, and the RoboMME permanence tasks.
